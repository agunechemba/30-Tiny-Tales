Chapter 1

The Baobab Puzzle

Computational Thinking

🧩

Amara opened the box and out tumbled one thousand puzzle pieces — a picture of a great baobab tree at sunset. “A thousand!” she gasped. “I will never finish this. Not ever.”

Mama sat down beside her under the mango tree. “Don’t look at the whole pile. First, find all the flat edge pieces.” So Amara did — and slowly the enormous pile turned into a neat little frame. Decomposition: one big job, broken into smaller jobs.

Then Amara noticed something. Orange pieces were the sky. Brown pieces were the trunk. Green pieces were the leaves. She sorted them into little piles without even trying. That was pattern recognition.

“Now,” said Mama, “do we need every single leaf? Or just the shape of the branches?” Amara grinned and put the boring bits aside. That was abstraction — keeping only what matters.

Last of all, Amara made a plan: edges first, then sky, then trunk, then leaves, then the animals at the foot of the tree. Step, step, step. That was an algorithm. By bedtime the puzzle was finished — and Amara had learned to think like a computer.

Big Idea

Computational thinking is four little habits: break it up, spot the patterns, keep only what matters, and write the steps in order.

Try It!

Pick a big job, like “clean the compound”. Break it into four smaller jobs. Find one pattern. Then write five steps, in order, that anyone could follow.
